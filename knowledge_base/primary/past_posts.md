We talk a lot about how employees cope with the pace of AI. 
We talk far less about the people trying to get in, who meet AI as a gate. 

What the evidence shows so far: 
→ In Mobley v. Workday, a US lawsuit, a man over 40 says he was rejected from more than 100 jobs at companies using the same screening platform. The age claim is allowed to proceed, but no court has found that Workday discriminated.

→ Bias in hiring predates AI. In a field experiment with nearly 4,000 companies, older women with matched qualifications were significantly less likely to get interview offers. Humans did that.  

→ Language models inherit a distorted picture of women at work. In a Nature study, ChatGPT wrote women’s résumés as younger and less experienced than men’s. That wasn’t a test of a real hiring product, but it shows what these models carry. 

Why human and tool bias are not the same problem: 
A biased recruiter is one gatekeeper among many. A screening tool shared by thousands of employers can apply the same judgment everywhere, with no one to persuade and no explanation. 

That is a wellbeing issue too. A rejection with a reason can be acted on. Silence can’t. 

In the EU, hiring AI is classed as high-risk, but the main obligations now apply from December 2027. Employers don’t have to wait: 

→ Test screening outcomes by age and gender
→ Review the rejections, not just the shortlist

Your Next Customer May Be an Agent. Is Your Business Ready?

For as long as businesses have had customers, the first contact has been a person. That assumption is starting to change.

On October 6, Meta and Sierra introduced the Personal Agent Protocol, an open specification for how AI agents act for consumers across websites, APIs and enterprise agents. It is still a draft, but the idea is clear: the agent identifies itself, starts as a guest, and reaches a customer’s account only after the customer signs in and grants view-only or change access. 

For businesses, that means decisions that used to be implicit now have to be made on purpose:
→ Which agents do you accept?
→ What can a guest agent see, and what can a signed-in agent change?
→ When does a human take over?
→ Can your team handle higher volumes? Service teams may see more requests as agents sign in and act for customers. 

The protocol answers who does this agent represent? It doesn’t answer is it acting well for them? A recent preprint found that a personal agent can become misaligned even when prompted to act in the user’s best interest. For refunds, cancellations and anything irreversible, that matters. 

Readiness doesn’t mean buying anything. It means answering these questions before the first agent shows up, and deciding who owns them.
